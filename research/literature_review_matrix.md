## 1. Overview and Taxonomic Categorization

To rigorously contextualize OneSensor within the broader academic landscape, the surveyed scholarly literature is organized into four distinct technical domains:
1. **Sensor-Cloud Infrastructure & Cloud-Centric Sensor Virtualization**: Frameworks abstracting physical transducers into cloud or middleware data services.
2. **Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) Simulation & Electrical Emulation**: Industrial and academic testbeds delivering physical electrical signals to real electronic control units (ECUs).
3. **Fault Injection Frameworks for Embedded Systems**: Methodologies for evaluating system resilience by corrupting hardware, memory, or signal state.
4. **Commodity Microcontroller Prototyping & Characterization**: Studies evaluating the timing, peripheral capabilities, and computational performance of modern dual-core SoCs (ESP32).

## 4. Key Scientific Insights from Literature Synthesis

1. **The Physical Abstraction Divide**: The existing sensor virtualization literature (SensIaaS, CA4IOT, Big-Sensor-Cloud) has almost exclusively addressed the cloud layer. It assumes that physical sensing hardware already exists and focuses on pooling data streams. OneSensor occupies the completely unaddressed inverse space: generating physical signals on demand to test down-stream embedded devices before physical sensors are deployed.
2. **The Economic Democratization of HIL**: Traditional HIL systems (National Instruments, dSPACE) achieve extraordinary precision but are economically inaccessible to students, makers, and small IoT enterprises. OneSensor proves that by accepting standard 8-bit DAC and 10-bit PWM resolutions, high-fidelity HIL testing ($R^2 = 0.9998$) can be accomplished using a $4 commodity SoC.
3. **The Sensor-Interface Fault Niche**: Prior fault injection tools operate either deep inside processor silicon (GOOFI-2, Magliano) or high up in software perception data (Elgharbawy). OneSensor uniquely injects deterministic faults right at the analog/PWM sensor wire interface, directly evaluating the target microcontroller's physical ADC conversion, digital timer capture, and noise immunity firmware.
